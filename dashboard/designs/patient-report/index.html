Exploration · Transparency in Coverage

Can patients use insurer price files to pick their own care?

Yes, for a narrow and valuable slice of care, but not from the raw files. A patient needs one plan, one service, and a short list of nearby providers, translated into what they will owe. The files hold the hardest ingredient, the negotiated rate. Everything else has to be added.

Prepared 29 Oct 2026 for the tic-crawler project. Grounded in the 29 Oct dry run (BCBS Michigan, BCBS Mississippi) and in published CMS rules. Market and tool details reflect public knowledge as of mid-2026 and were not re-checked live. Dollar figures in the worked example are illustrative.

Bottom line

What this data is good for

  • Best fit: planned, shoppable services where prices vary a lot between nearby providers and the patient has time to choose. Think MRI and CT scans, lab panels, colonoscopy, sleep studies, physical therapy, and outpatient surgeries such as knee arthroscopy or cataract removal.
  • Biggest win: patients on high-deductible plans, and anyone who has not met their deductible, because they pay close to the full negotiated rate. A $450 versus $2,100 MRI is the patient's own money.
  • Hard limits: the files say nothing about quality, wait times, or whether a doctor is taking patients. Rates are per billing code, while real episodes involve several codes and several bills. Emergencies are not shoppable.
  • Product shape: a patient tool runs on a small, pre-built index (plan × service × provider), not on the multi-terabyte files. tic-crawler already does the first step that index needs: finding the right files for each plan.

The first rollout is for people covered through employers that Borislow Insurance advises. So the "patient" here is an employee or family member on an employer group plan, and the broker and HR team are the way to reach them. That changes what to build first.

Patient questions

What the files can and can't answer

Each row is a question a patient actually asks. The verdict is about TiC data alone, before joining anything else.

Patient questionTiC aloneWhat fills the gap
Which in-network imaging center near me charges my plan least for a knee MRI?YesProvider address and specialty from NPPES (the NPI registry); distance from the patient's ZIP.
How much will I actually pay?PartlyThe patient's deductible, coinsurance, copay, and how much they have met so far. Only the insurer or the patient knows this.
Is this doctor in my network?PartlyA rate listed for an NPI suggests a contract, but files list NPIs under a tax ID (TIN) and can include clinicians who no longer practice there. Check the insurer's provider directory.
What does the whole episode cost (surgeon, facility, anesthesia, imaging)?PartlyAn episode definition: which codes usually go together. Some contracts are bundled and appear as a single rate; most are not.
Which plan should I pick at open enrollment if I care about my own doctors?PartlyEach plan's benefit design (premium, deductible, coinsurance) from the plan's Summary of Benefits; the patient's expected services.
Is this provider any good?NoCMS Care Compare, Leapfrog safety grades, state outcome reports, specialty registries. Coverage is uneven outside hospitals.
Can I get an appointment soon?NoScheduling data from the provider. Not public.
Was my bill right?YesThe patient's own Explanation of Benefits. Compare its allowed amount to the posted rate for that code and provider.
What will out-of-network care cost me?PartlyAllowed-amount files show historical out-of-network payments, but only where a payer had 20 or more claims for that code and provider, so most are near-empty.
Is paying cash cheaper than using insurance?PartlyThe provider's cash price, from hospital price transparency files or by asking. Cash payments may not count toward the deductible.
The core calculation

From negotiated rate to “what I'll pay”

The number in the file is what the insurer and provider agreed the service is worth. The patient's bill depends on where they are in their plan year. A tool has to do this math, and has to be honest that it is an estimate.

deductible_part = min(rate, deductible_remaining) after_deductible = rate − deductible_part patient_owes = deductible_part + coinsurance × after_deductible (or the copay, if the service has one) patient_owes = min(patient_owes, out_of_pocket_max_remaining)

Illustrative figures, not taken from the BCBS files. The gap between freestanding imaging centers and hospital outpatient departments for the same scan is one of the most consistent patterns in published price-transparency research. Once the deductible is met, the same choice saves this patient 20% of the difference instead of all of it, which is why the tool matters most early in the plan year.

From the dry run

What the files look like up close

The 29 Oct crawl gives a feel for the raw material. BCBS Michigan's two tables of contents pointed to 812 files totaling about 188 GB (518 files in the employer TOC, 294 in the group TOC). Run 1 also catalogued BCBS Mississippi, bringing the referenced in-network data to roughly 293 GB for just two Blues plans. A national carrier is far larger.

  • Scale is the first problem. No patient app can read these files live. They have to be parsed once per month into a compact index. Most of the bytes are repeated provider lists and rates for codes a patient would never shop for.
  • Plans are grouped coarsely. One in-network file covers many plans (the manifest keeps up to 50 plan names per file). A patient knows their plan's name or the ID on their card; matching that to a file is a real UX step, and tic-crawler's manifest is the lookup table for it.
  • Payers bend the spec. BCBS Michigan writes HIOS, EIN and Group where the schema requires lower case, which failed validation 43 and 10 times. Harmless here, but a sign that every payer will need its own cleanup rules.
  • Out-of-network data is thin. The BCBS Michigan PPO allowed-amounts file is 170 KB against tens of gigabytes of in-network rates, because of the 20-claim reporting threshold.
  • Some files are off limits. BCBS Mississippi's TOCs are disallowed by robots.txt and BCBS Kansas returned 403. A patient product needs a coverage map so it can say "we don't have your plan" instead of showing nothing.

Known quality hazards inside the rates

  • Ghost rates: contracts list every code for every clinician under a tax ID, so a dermatologist may "have" a rate for cardiac surgery. Filter rates by the provider's specialty and by codes they plausibly bill.
  • Non-dollar arrangements: some rates are a percent of billed charges or "derived" amounts, which can't be turned into a patient price without the provider's chargemaster.
  • Place of service: the same code has different professional and facility rates. An estimate for a hospital visit must add the facility fee.
  • Freshness: files are monthly. CMS-9882-F adds quarterly update requirements from March 2027, which helps with directory churn.

Product concepts

Five ideas, most feasible first

Shop one scan

Feasible now · narrow scope

Pick your plan, ZIP, and one of ~30 shoppable services. See in-network sites ranked by estimated cost after you enter your deductible. Start with one state and imaging plus labs, where ghost rates are rare and savings are large.

Check my bill

Feasible now · small data need

Type or photograph the codes and allowed amounts from an EOB; the tool compares them to the posted rate for that provider and drafts a question to the insurer. Needs only the rate lookup, not distance or quality.

Open enrollment calculator

Medium · seasonal, high value

For an employer with several plans: "my doctors plus a typical year for me" gives a total-cost estimate per plan. Needs plan-to-file matching (tic-crawler's manifest) and benefit details from each plan's Summary of Benefits.

Referral helper for clinicians

Medium · reaches patients at the decision

The patient often picks where to go at the moment a doctor writes the referral. A list of lower-cost in-network sites for the patient's plan, handed over at that point, reaches more people than a website they have to find.

Price plus quality map

Harder · biggest promise

Join rates to Care Compare and Leapfrog so patients see cost and quality together. Quality data covers hospitals well and outpatient clinics poorly, so this works first for surgery and childbirth.

Design principles

UX lessons for a patient-facing tool

  • Ask for the plan first, in the patient's words: insurer, plan name from the card, or employer. Everything else depends on it.
  • Use plain service names ("MRI of the knee") and map them to codes behind the scenes. Patients don't know CPT codes, and many shoppable services span several.
  • Show a range and a confidence level, never a single precise dollar figure. Say what is included and what is not, such as facility fees.
  • Keep deductible and spending inputs on the patient's device. That keeps the tool free of member data, which matches tic-crawler's own rule.
  • Tell the patient to confirm network status with the insurer before booking, and say plainly when their plan isn't covered.
  • Lead with a cheaper option that is close and well rated. Price alone pushes people toward places they don't trust.
Where tic-crawler fits

What it would take from here

tic-crawler today answers "which files cover which plans, and are they reachable." A patient tool needs four more layers. None of them are built, and each would need the owner's sign-off because they mean new storage and more than 10 GB per run.

Cheapest real test: take one BCBS Michigan in-network file, extract rates for about ten imaging and lab codes, join NPPES, and see whether the price spread across Michigan sites is large and believable. That single file answers whether the "Shop one scan" idea is worth building, for the cost of one download. For the first rollout, run this test on a Borislow client's plan instead (see above).
